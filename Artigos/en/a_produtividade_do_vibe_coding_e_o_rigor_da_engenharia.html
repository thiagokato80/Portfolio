The Productivity of "Vibe Coding" and the Rigour of Engineering: where AI accelerates (and where it fails)

AI tools quadruple the feeling of productivity and are genuine accelerators of execution. But architecture and debugging keep handing sovereignty back to the engineer — and that is what separates the vibe coder from the production engineer.

October 15, 2025 · 3 min read

The new deal between the engineer and the AI

The arrival of LLM-based coding extensions, such as the Gemini and Copilot tools, opened an era I call "vibe coding". The experience is undeniably exciting: the code flows, the syntax materialises, and the sense of productivity quadruples. These tools are massive accelerators for development, making the act of coding less tedious.

In my recent project architecting a general AI agent in Python, I used these tools actively. What I learned, however, is that the AI is a brilliant execution assistant but a flawed architect. True engineering mastery shows up precisely in the machine's blind spots.

The productivity jump: AI as an execution accelerator

My productivity shot up on tasks that used to consume unnecessary cognitive energy:

  • Boilerplate and syntax: the AI turned class and method stubs, and repetitive Python syntax, into a near-instant process. In a multi-agent system, where the architecture is complex but the syntax connecting modules can be monotonous, the AI freed me up.
  • Data handling with Pandas and SQL: instead of pausing to recall the exact function for a complex groupby or a specific merge, the AI translates my prompt into working Python in seconds. That let me spend time where the value actually is: security logic and high-level orchestration.

In that sense, the AI is a master of code translation. It sets the pace, letting a senior engineer spend 80% of their time on high-level architecture rather than low-level execution.

Where the AI fails: the engineer's sovereignty

Despite that ease, continued use reinforced one crucial truth: the solution and the technical rigour always depend on the engineer. The tools failed me at two critical points.

1. Architectural innovation and solution design

The agent required a non-trivial solution: a distributed orchestration system, with specialist agents and a metadata-driven data analysis mechanism to enforce security and scalability.

No AI tool suggested that architecture. The idea, the decomposition of the problem and the design of the solution came entirely from me. The AI only becomes useful after the engineer defines what should be built and why.

2. The ordeal of debugging complex systems

The moment of greatest frustration, and also of greatest learning, happens in debugging. In integrated systems — Python talking to an ERP, an LLM and Teams, for example — subtle logic bugs and integration failures are inevitable.

At those moments, the tool's suggested fixes turn out, most of the time, to be incorrect, superficial, generic and often repetitive — with the AI trying the same solution that had already failed.

The answer requires traditional engineering rigour: trace the failure through the stack, read specific documentation and, often, arrive at a fix fundamentally different from what the AI advised.

Debugging is the trial by fire that separates the vibe coder from the production engineer. The bug hands sovereignty back to the human, demanding deep knowledge of the system and the language.

From vibe coding to augmented engineering

AI tools transformed coding, not engineering. They are excellent at syntax and execution, but responsibility for the rigour, security and architecture of a production system remains entirely the engineer's.

Using AI teaches a lesson in focus: spend your energy and your domain knowledge on thinking through the solution and guaranteeing rigour in the delivery. Let the AI do the heavy lifting of typing. That is how we reach augmented engineering: the speed of the machine joined to the intelligence and responsibility of the human.
